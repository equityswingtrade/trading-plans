Last week, marked honestly

Every one of the 28 candidates from the 7 September run was recorded as one assumed contract at the quoted mark. Here is each one priced against the 11 September close. The October options are still live, so these are mark-to-market numbers, not settled ones — except Amgen, whose stop level was breached, which fixes the loss.

The headline: −$4,170 across 28, and the wrong screens won

The eight-name cash-secured put plan — the thing you would actually have traded — is −$829 on $73,550 of cash, which is -1.13% in five sessions, or -0.83% of a $100,000 account. Survivable. The uncomfortable part is the breakdown: names picked by both screens lost $2,814 with only 4 of 10 up, the technical-only group lost $1,722, and the two groups I had least confidence in — volatility-only and the failed-both watch list — came in +$347 and +$19. One week is not evidence of anything. It is still the opposite of what the method predicts, and pretending otherwise would make this report useless.

Amgen — stopped, −$2,685

It entered with the highest technical score of all 260 names (9.14) and a clean IV/RV of 1.24, then fell 13.7% from $437.23 to $377.33, straight through its $383.92 stop. It accounts for 64% of the entire week’s loss. It was not in the cash-secured put plan — at $41,000 of cash it did not fit a $100k account — but it was in the spread plan, where the 410/390 would now be at or near its $1,635 maximum loss.

The screen has since flipped it: technical score 4.08, IV/RV 0.66. It now fails both tests. A 9.14 describes the past; the stop was the only forward-looking protection.

Dollar Tree — in the money, −$598

131.42 to 118.17, a 10.1% fall. Below the 125 strike, so on the assignment track, but still above its $111.31 stop, so the position stands. If it expires here you take the shares at $125 with a $121.83 basis and write the 125 call for roughly $5.55 a cycle — about 4.6% on basis per cycle, which recovers the current mark in a little over one cycle.

This is the wheel working as designed. Assignment is not the failure mode; assignment below the stop is.

Valero — +$575, the best position twice running

The name last run identified as the single best risk-adjusted trade in the sweep rose 5.4% and made more than any other line. It comes back this week with an IV/RV of 1.71, the second-highest in the new pool, and it is top of the monthly plan again.

Qualcomm — +$210, and the screen said no

It scored 4.16 technically last run and was left out of every plan. It then rose 7.8%. The technical floor cost money on this one, and the fair way to record that is to say so: the exclusion was wrong, in this instance, by $210.

It scores 5.20 this week with IV/RV 1.24 — still below the plan threshold, still on the watch list. The judgement has not changed; the confidence in it should be lower.

What I am changing because of this. Nothing about the two screens — one week of 28 positions cannot overturn a volatility-risk-premium result, and reacting to it would be exactly the mistake the method exists to prevent. Three things do change: names with earnings inside the expiry window are now excluded from the monthly plan (last week’s book had no such rule, and the highest IV/RV readings this week are all earnings artefacts); sector concentration is capped at two names and the energy complex at four, because Valero, Marathon and Phillips 66 are one trade in three tickers; and every candidate now shows its bid/ask as a percentage of the mark, because several of last week’s marks were not fillable.

Tesla, Nvidia, SpaceX

You asked why three of the biggest names on the tape are not in the list. The three answers are different, and one of them is my mistake rather than the screen’s.

TSLA — $365.44

Rejected by the trend filter

Tesla closes at $365.44 against a 200-day moving average of $398.93 — 8.4% below it. The first-stage filter requires price above the 200-day, so it never reaches the option pricing stage. Its technical score is 1.61: it broke the 200-day on volume inside the last month, it sits 34% up its 52-week range, and its trend efficiency is 0.07, meaning six months of movement that went essentially nowhere. It is liquid ($11.4bn a day) and it would fit the account at $33,500 a contract. The chart is the problem.

what would change it  a close back above ~$399 with the 200-day turning up

NVDA — $218.29

Passes the chart, fails the pay

Nvidia clears every stage-1 filter and scores 7.33 technically — rank 52 of 237, with a rising 200-day, higher lows, and a 7.0% pullback on lighter volume. The option chain is the best in this report: 30,390 open interest on the October 205 put and a bid/ask 2.9% of the mark. And then the arithmetic: 33.5% implied against 40.8% realised, a ratio of 0.82. You would be paid 18% less than the stock has actually been moving. That is the one thing this screen will not do — and it is the same verdict as last week, when the ratio was 0.84.

what would change it  implied volatility above roughly 41% while the chart holds  →  or take the 205/195 spread at 21.1% on $826 of risk if you want the exposure regardless

SPCX — $151.21

My universe was wrong. Now it is in, and it fails both tests

SpaceX was not rejected last week — it was never scored. The 480-name universe was built from an S&P 500 constituents file plus a Nasdaq-100 add list, and SPCX is in neither, so the scan never saw it. That is a universe-construction gap and it is worth naming plainly: the pool was a proxy for “big-cap”, not the thing itself. It is fully listed, has 251 daily bars and trades $12.6bn a day — more than Tesla.

It is now in a 506-name universe alongside 29 other large caps that the index lists were missing (CoreWeave, Reddit, Snowflake, Shopify, Spotify, TSMC, ASML, Coinbase, Robinhood, MercadoLibre and others). Here is what the screen says now: it clears stage 1, but the technical score is 2.50 — rank 226 of 237, because it broke its 200-day on volume inside the last month, and the October 140 put prices 51.4% implied against 83.4% realised: a ratio of 0.62, the worst in the book. Its 3.18% monthly yield is the fattest number on the page and the thinnest real edge on it.

the lesson  a missing name is a data problem and gets fixed; a 0.62 ratio is an answer

All three in one line

Tesla fails the chart. Nvidia passes the chart and fails the price of the option. SpaceX was missing from the data and fails both once included. The only one of the three I would trade is Nvidia, and only as a defined-risk spread, and only because you asked — not because the screen says to.

What $300,000 changes

At $100,000 the 20% concentration limit capped a single cash-secured put at $20,000, which ruled out most of the interesting names. At $300,000 the cap is $60,000, and every name in this report clears it. The constraint has moved: it is no longer capital, it is how many names actually pass both screens with a chain you can trade. That is why positions are now sized in multiple contracts rather than one.

cash to secure one put same trade as a credit spread

The new arithmetic
Max single position $60,000. Low-volatility core $225,000 (75%), high-volatility sleeve $75,000 (25%, unchanged as a proportion). The monthly plan deploys 96.8% of the account across 18 contracts.
Scaling contracts, not standards
The temptation at $300k is to reach further down the ranking. The better use of the capital is more contracts in the names that already pass — three of Coca-Cola, four of Intel, two of Conoco — each still inside the 20% cap. Reaching down the list is how you end up short puts on Biogen with 49 contracts of open interest.
Spreads still earn their place
The whole spread book risks $14,945 — 5.0% of the account — for $3,555 of credit, against $290,500 of cash for $5,542. That is the efficiency argument, and it is real. What you give up is the wheel: no assignment, no shares, no covered call, no recovery.
Minimum account, per name
Still cash ÷ 20%. Microsoft’s $47,500 put needs a $237,500 account; Regeneron’s would need $385,000 and is not in this report. Elevance at $39,000 needs $195,000. All now inside $300,000.

When it breaks

failure mode 01

Assigned — sell the call

Only happens on the cash-secured side, and it is the designed outcome. Each candidate carries its own number: Valero at $350.45 basis writes the 360 call for about $22.97 a cycle (6.6% on basis), Intel $7.76 on an $89.57 basis (8.7%), Arista $12.84 (7.1%), Coca-Cola $2.19 (2.6%). Never write the call below basis. Dollar Tree from last week is the live example: assigned at $125, basis $121.83, write the 125.

trigger  stock below the strike at 4pm on expiry Friday  →  take the shares, write the call next session at or above basis

failure mode 02

The strike breaks early

Roll down and out for a net credit only, twice at most. A spread rolls the same way but the long leg costs you on the way out — check the net before assuming a roll is available.

trigger  short-put delta reaches −0.50, or a close below the 60-day low in the stop table

failure mode 03

The assigned name keeps falling

This is what happened to Amgen, and it cost $2,685 — 64% of the week’s entire loss on one name. The stop table gives the level and the dollar cost per name, and how many covered-call cycles earn it back. The largest single stop line this week is Intel at $3,665 across four contracts. On a spread there is nothing to decide: below the long strike the loss is fixed and the position is done.

trigger  assigned stock closes below the hard stop  →  close it. No averaging down, no matter what the technical score said on entry

failure mode 04

Everything falls together

At −10% the put book loses $4,178 and the spread book $7,163. At −30% the put book is −$66,528, 22% of the account. The sector caps exist for this: without them the top of the ranking would have been five refiners and a copper miner, all one bet on the same commodity cycle.

trigger  VIX doubles from entry, or the S&P closes below its 200-day  →  stop opening new positions of either kind

failure mode 05

The fill never comes

Bigger this run, not smaller, because $300,000 reaches further into thin chains. Biogen’s October 200 quotes $0.80 bid against $4.20 ask — a spread 136% of the mark on 49 open interest, and its long leg quotes zero-bid with a delta of −0.003, so there is no spread either. Elevance is 92% of the mark, Verisign has 19 contracts of open interest, Metlife’s long leg is a one-sided market. Every candidate now shows bid/ask as a percentage of the mark: green under 20%, amber to 40%, red above. A two-leg spread order crosses two of those.

trigger  bid/ask wider than a third of the credit, or open interest under ~100  →  limit at mid, no chasing, and skip it if it does not fill

failure mode 06

The high ratio is an earnings artefact

New rule this run. State Street at 2.09, BNY Mellon at 1.84, Bank of America at 1.83 and Johnson & Johnson at 1.37 are the four best-looking ratios in the sweep, and all four report between 13 and 15 October — one to three days before expiry. The implied volatility is high because a known binary event sits inside the contract’s life. That is not a mispricing you are harvesting; it is a risk you are being paid a fair price to take, with no edge in it. All four are excluded from the monthly plan.

trigger  an earnings date inside the expiry window  →  monthly excluded; sell the weekly that expires first, or wait until the day after the report

Data & method

  • Universe: 506 names — S&P 500, Nasdaq-100 additions, and 30 large caps the index lists were missing, including SPCX, CRWV, RDDT, SNOW, SHOP, SPOT, TSM, ASML, COIN, HOOD and MELI. All 506 carry a full year of daily bars to the 11 September close. 237 cleared stage 1; 36 were priced against live chains.
  • Account $300,000; single-position concentration limit 20%, so a maximum of $60,000 of cash per name. Minimum account per name = cash ÷ 20%.
  • Strikes chosen by live delta, not modelled: every October leg is the listed strike whose quoted delta is closest to −0.25, and every September leg closest to −0.20, from the actual chain. Where the strike grid is coarse the realised delta is shown and differs from target.
  • Volume profile: each session’s volume spread evenly across the price bands its high–low range covers, binned into 44 bands over the visible window; the point of control is the busiest band and the value area the 70% of volume around it.
  • Quotes, chains, Greeks, implied volatility, open interest and earnings dates: live pulls from the connected brokerage account at the Fri 11 Sep 2026 close. Every put price, including both spread legs, is a live mark.
  • Covered-call premiums are modelled (Black–Scholes, 35 DTE) at the implied volatility quoted on each name’s live forward call — 20 November where listed, 18 December for MPC, VLO, EOG, OKE, APA, BNY and BIIB, which have no November expiry.
  • Week-one marks use the 11 September close against the 4 September entry marks. October contracts are still live, so those figures are mark-to-market, not settled; Amgen is treated as resolved because its stop level was breached.
  • Technical rules from cash-secured put timing guidance and the 20-MA pullback framework; delta and duration from the ApexVol five-year wheel backtest.
